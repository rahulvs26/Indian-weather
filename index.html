2 · The Madden–Julian Oscillation, explained

An interactive reference article: the tropical intraseasonal oscillation that governs the rhythm of the Indian monsoon — what it is, how it is measured, what each phase does, and what it is regularly confused with. Tap any of the eight sectors of the phase diagram.

The Madden–Julian Oscillation (MJO) is a cycle of organised convection that travels eastward across the tropics in roughly 30 to 60 days, returning to where it began. It was first described by Madden and Julian in 1972 from Pacific records, and it propagates over the Indian Ocean, the Maritime Continent and the Pacific in turn. Because it repeatedly brings organised convection close to the subcontinent every few weeks, it is the reason an active southwest monsoon is not continuous but arrives in spells.

It is not the monsoon. The monsoon is the seasonal reversal of wind caused by the land–ocean heating contrast and is broadly in place from June to September. The MJO is an intraseasonal wobble on top of it, active for a few weeks and then gone. Treating the two as the same thing is the single most common error in amateur monsoon forecasting.

How it works. Warm water in the equatorial oceans heats and moistens the lower troposphere; deep convection develops; the latent heating excites a Rossby wave response in the upper troposphere. That wave travels eastward, and when it reaches the next warm pool it reinforces convection there. The cycle repeats. The whole system is limited to the warm Indo-Pacific belt — over the cold air of Eurasia and over land the wave is absorbed, which is why the cycle resets over the Maritime Continent.
